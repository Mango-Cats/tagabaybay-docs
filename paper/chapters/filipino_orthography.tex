\section{Filipino Orthography and System Design}\label{sec:filipino_orthography}

The orthographic properties of Filipino directly motivate the three core
components of our nativization pipeline: phonetic vowel resolution, rule-based
consonant mapping, and syllable repair. This section describes these properties
and explains how each shapes the corresponding stage of the system.

\subsection{Vowel Spelling and Phonetic Resolution}

Filipino orthography uses five vowel letters: \emph{a}, \emph{e}, \emph{i},
\emph{o}, and \emph{u}, because English's vowel inventory is
larger, multiple English vowels must collapse onto a single Filipino letter
when a word is nativized~\cite{almario2014}. For instance, English /\textipa{\ae}/ (``cat'') and
/\textipa{A:}/ (``father'') are both written as \emph{a} in Filipino. This
many-to-one mapping creates ambiguity that cannot be resolved from English
spelling alone: ``a'' in ``make'' /\textipa{eI}/ becomes \emph{ey}, while ``a''
in ``cat'' /\textipa{\ae}/ becomes \emph{a}. This motivates our phonetic
resolution stage, which invokes a G2P component to determine which English
vowel sound is present, enabling correct mapping to Filipino letters.

Further complicating vowel adaptation, the pairs (\emph{e}, \emph{i}) and
(\emph{o}, \emph{u}) are widely treated as interchangeable in many lexical
contexts, a convention rooted in the historical allophonic status of these
pairs in Filipino~\cite{schachter1972}. In practice, native speakers often
accept either spelling variant without perceiving an error. This variation is
especially common in borrowed vocabulary, where prescriptive sources do not
always uniquely specify a choice. This allographic flexibility motivates our
additional evaluation conditions, in which (\emph{e}, \emph{i}, \emph{y}) and
(\emph{o}, \emph{u}) are treated as equivalent, providing a more linguistically
realistic assessment of system performance.

\subsection{Consonant Letter Correspondences and Context-Sensitive Rules}

For the fifteen consonant letters of the native Filipino
alphabet~\cite{llamzon1966}, spelling correspondences between English and
Filipino are largely predictable through one-to-one mappings (\emph{b}
\(\mapsto\) \emph{b}, \emph{m} \(\mapsto\) \emph{m}, etc.). However, English
loanwords frequently contain sounds that have no native Filipino letter,
including /f/, /v/, /z/, /\textipa{S}/, /\textipa{tS}/, and /\textipa{dZ}/.
Prescriptive guidelines~\cite{almario2014,llamzon1966} provide standard written
forms for these cases: /\textipa{tS}/ is written as \emph{ts}, /\textipa{dZ}/
as \emph{dy}, and /\textipa{S}/ as \emph{s} or \emph{sy}. These systematic
correspondences motivate our context-free mapping rules, which handle the
majority of consonant adaptations through direct substitution.

Some graphemes, however, require context-sensitive treatment. The letter
\emph{c}, for example, maps to \emph{s} before \emph{i} or \emph{e} (``cinema''
\(\mapsto\) \emph{sine}) but to \emph{k} elsewhere (``computer'' \(\mapsto\)
\emph{kompyuter}). Similarly, doubled consonants are simplified (``addict''
\(\mapsto\) \emph{adik}), and word-final silent \emph{e} is dropped (``make''
\(\mapsto\) \emph{meyk}). These environment-dependent patterns motivate our
context-sensitive rule layer, which inspects neighboring graphemes to select
the appropriate mapping.

\subsection{Syllable-Level Spelling Repairs and Output Normalization}

Filipino spelling strongly favors syllable patterns of the form CV or
CVC~\cite{llamzon1966,magpale2024}. When direct grapheme-to-grapheme mapping would produce
a consonant cluster that violates these patterns, such as the ``-bl'' in a
naive adaptation of ``rumble'', prescriptive convention inserts a vowel letter to
break up the illegal sequence, preserving all source consonants
in the spelling, \emph{``rambol''}~\cite{almario2014}. This phonotactic constraint motivates our
output normalization stage, which detects word-final consonant clusters ending
in \emph{l} or \emph{r} and inserts epenthetic \emph{o} where needed. The
normalization pass also collapses contiguous duplicate graphemes and removes
redundant affricate prefixes, ensuring that the final output conforms to
Filipino orthographic conventions even when individual rule applications
produce intermediate artifacts.
