\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Implementation}
The system is implemented in Rust. Vowel resolution relies on a
G2P backend provided by the
\texttt{phonemizer} library~\cite{bernard2021}, which wraps the
eSpeak-NG speech synthesizer and is invoked as a Python subprocess.

\subsubsection{Rule Construction}

The nativization rules were not learned from data but were instead
hand-authored and grounded in a combination of official orthographic
standards and descriptive phonological literature. The primary authority
for Filipino spelling conventions is the KWF \emph{Manwal sa Masinop na
Pagsulat}~\cite{almario2014}, which prescribes how borrowed consonants,
vowels, and consonant clusters should be rendered in Filipino orthography.
Context-free rules are direct encodings of the correspondences
listed in the KWF manual.

Context-sensitive rules were derived from two complementary sources.
First, well-documented English G2P regularities were encoded as conditional
mappings. Second, descriptions of Filipino phonotactic constraints
in~\cite{llamzon1966,schachter1972} informed cluster-repair strategies:
for instance, the epenthetic vowel insertion that breaks up illegal onset
clusters follows the canonical CV syllable template of Filipino described
in~\cite{llamzon1966}.

Phonetic vowel-mapping rules were informed by studies of Philippine
English phonology~\cite{llamzon1997,tayao2004,magpale2026,magpale2025,magpale_hong_2024_sc},
which describe how the vowel inventory of Philippine English collapses
the larger English system into roughly five
categories. These studies provided the empirical basis for the mapping
from English IPA vowels to the five-vowel Filipino inventory.

\subsection{Gold Standard}

The gold standard corpus was constructed by native Filipino speakers and
comprises 1,484 English--Filipino loanword pairs organized into four
datasets spanning Philippine domains where English loanwords are commonly used.

For every entry, the annotator determined the correct Filipino nativized
form by applying the spelling conventions prescribed in~\cite{almario2014}. 
Where the manual did not prescribe a
specific nativization, annotators consulted descriptive accounts of
Tagalog phonology~\cite{llamzon1966,schachter1972} and Philippine English
phonology~\cite{llamzon1997,tayao2004,magpale2026,magpale2025,magpale_hong_2024_sc}
to determine the most plausible nativized form under the five-vowel
system.

To validate corpus quality, inter-annotator agreement was measured using
a dedicated validation script. For each dataset, a random subsample of up
to 60\% of entries was presented to a second native-Filipino annotator,
who independently produced a nativized form for each English source word
without seeing the first annotator's output. Agreement is reported as the
percentage of entries on which both annotators produced identical nativized
forms.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The primary evaluation metric is Character Error Rate (CER), computed as
the Levenshtein edit distance between the system output and the gold
standard reference, normalized by the total character count of the
reference across the entire corpus:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CER} = \frac{\sum_{i} \mathrm{lev}(\hat{y}_i,\, y_i)}
    {\sum_{i} |y_i|}
    \times 100\%
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}_i$ is the system's nativized form of the $i$-th word, $y_i$ is
the gold standard reference, $\mathrm{lev}(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes
character-level Levenshtein distance, and $|y_i|$ is the character length
of the reference. CER is preferred over word-level accuracy because
nativization errors are often minor (e.g., a single vowel substitution),
and a word-level metric would treat a one-character discrepancy as harshly
as a completely incorrect output.

We set the acceptance threshold at CER $< 20\%$, meaning that, on average,
fewer than one in five reference characters is affected by an edit. We
additionally report CER under two relaxed evaluation conditions motivated
by a well-known property of Filipino phonology: the vowels /e/ and /i/
and the vowels /o/ and /u/ are historically allophonic in
Tagalog~\cite{schachter1972}. Because native speakers often accept both
members of each pair interchangeably in nativized forms, we compute CER
variants in which \{e, i, y\} are treated as equivalent and \{o, u\} are
treated as equivalent, as well as a combined condition in which both
equivalences hold simultaneously. These relaxed metrics provide a more
linguistically informed picture of system performance by discounting
errors that would not be perceived as incorrect by most Filipino speakers.

Beyond the aggregate CER, the evaluation framework produces per-dataset
CER scores and a ranked inventory of the most frequent character-level
error patterns, enabling targeted diagnosis of systematic rule
failures.
