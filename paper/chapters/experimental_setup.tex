\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Implementation}
The system is implemented in Rust. The G2P backend for vowel resolution is
provided by the \texttt{phonemizer} library~\cite{bernard2021}, which wraps the
eSpeak-NG speech synthesizer and is invoked as a Python subprocess. The G2P
component is called only when the current token is a vowel grapheme whose
nativized form cannot be determined from spelling context alone; for all other
tokens, the system operates entirely within the Rust process.

The nativization rules described in~\cref{sec:filipino_orthography} were
hand-authored, not learned from data. Context-free and context-sensitive rules
were drawn from~\cite{almario2014} and from descriptive accounts of Filipino
phonotactics~\cite{llamzon1966,schachter1972}. Vowel-mapping rules were
informed by studies of Filipino
phonology~\cite{llamzon1966,llamzon1997,tayao2004,magpale2026,magpale2025,magpale_hong_2024_sc},
which document how the larger English vowel system reduces to roughly five
categories in Filipino usage.

\subsection{Gold Standard}

\subsubsection{Construction}
The gold standard corpus was constructed by native Filipino speakers and
comprises 1,484 English--Filipino loanword pairs organized into four datasets
spanning Philippine domains where English loanwords are commonly used.

For every entry, the annotator determined the correct Filipino nativized form
by applying the spelling conventions prescribed in~\cite{almario2014}. Where
the manual did not prescribe a specific nativization, annotators consulted
descriptive accounts of Filipino
phonology~\cite{llamzon1966,schachter1972,llamzon1997,tayao2004,magpale2026,magpale2025,magpale_hong_2024_sc}
to determine the most plausible nativized form under the five-vowel system.

\subsubsection{Inter-Annotator Agreement}
To validate corpus quality, inter-annotator agreement was measured using a
dedicated validation script. For each dataset, a random subsample of 60\% of
entries was presented to a second native-Filipino annotator, who independently
produced a nativized form for each English source word without seeing the first
annotator's output. Agreement is reported as the percentage of entries on which
both annotators produced identical nativized forms.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
The primary evaluation metric is Character Error Rate (CER), computed as the
Levenshtein edit distance between the system output and the gold standard
reference, normalized by the total character count of the reference across the
entire corpus:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CER} = \frac{\sum_{i} \mathrm{lev}(\hat{y}_i,\, y_i)}
    {\sum_{i} |y_i|}
    \times 100\%
\end{equation}
where \(\hat{y}_i\) is the system's nativized form of the \(i\)-th word, \(y_i\) is
the gold standard reference, \(\mathrm{lev}(\cdot,\cdot)\) denotes
character-level Levenshtein distance, and \(|y_i|\) is the character length of
the reference. CER is preferred over word-level accuracy because nativization
errors are often minor, and a
word-level metric would treat a one-character discrepancy as harshly as a
completely incorrect output.

We set the acceptance threshold at CER \(< 20\%\), meaning that on average
fewer than one in five reference characters is affected by an edit. We
additionally report CER under two relaxed evaluation conditions motivated by
the \emph{e}/\emph{i} and \emph{o}/\emph{u} allographic variation described
in~\cref{sec:filipino_orthography}: because native speakers often accept both
members of each pair interchangeably in nativized forms, we compute CER
variants in which \{e, i, y\} are treated as equivalent and \{o, u\} are
treated as equivalent, as well as a combined condition in which both
equivalences hold simultaneously. These relaxed metrics provide a more
linguistically informed picture of system performance by discounting errors
that would not be perceived as incorrect by most Filipino speakers.
