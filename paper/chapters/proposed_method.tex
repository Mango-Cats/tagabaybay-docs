\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:method}

The system performs orthographic nativization of English loanwords through a
four-stage pipeline: graphemic tokenization, rule-based grapheme mapping,
phonetic resolution via G2P lookup for ambiguous vowels, and output
normalization. A single priority-ordered rewrite cascade, applied left to
right, carries out both grapheme mapping and phonetic resolution; the G2P
transcription it consults is computed once, up front. The input is the English
orthographic form; the output is the Filipino form prescribed
by~\cite{almario2014}. The linguistic properties
of~\cref{sec:filipino_orthography} determine the content of each stage.
\Cref{fig:pipeline} gives the architecture, and~\cref{alg:nativize} states the
top-level control flow.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
      \caption{Orthographic Nativization}\label{alg:nativize}
      \begin{algorithmic}[1]
            \Require English loanword $w$
            \Ensure Filipino orthographic form
            \State $G \gets \Call{Tokenize}{w}$
            \State $P \gets \Call{G2P}{w}$
            \State $R \gets \langle\,\rangle$; \quad $i \gets 1$
            \While{$i \le |G|$}
            \For{$\rho \in \langle \textsc{Sensitive}, \textsc{Phonetic}, \textsc{Free}\rangle$}
            \If{$(r, c) \gets \rho(G, P, i)$ \textbf{succeeds}}
            \State $R \gets R \cdot r$; \quad $i \gets i + c$; \quad \textbf{break}
            \EndIf
            \EndFor
            \EndWhile
            \State \Return \Call{Normalize}{$R$, $P$}
      \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Every rule returns a pair $(r, c)$: a (possibly empty) \emph{sequence} of
Filipino graphemes and the number of English graphemes \emph{consumed}.
Position advances by $c$ so a single rule may rewrite a multi-grapheme span.
For example, \gr{ck} $\mapsto$ \gr{k} consumes two.

\subsection{Graphemic Tokenization}

English's deep orthography contains multi-letter sequences that behave as
single units, so the input is first segmented into graphemes. The system
recognizes digraphs (e.g., \gr{ph}, \gr{ch}), trigraphs (e.g., \gr{ore},
\gr{tch}), and tetragraphs (\gr{augh}, \gr{ough}, \gr{eigh}). Writing $M_k$ for
the inventory of length $k$, tokenization is greedy longest-match
(\cref{alg:tokenize}): at each position the longest matching multigraph is
emitted, falling back to a single character. Thus ``straight'' segments as
\gr{s-t-r-a-igh-t} and ``phishing'' as \gr{ph-i-sh-i-ng}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
      \caption{Graphemic Tokenization}\label{alg:tokenize}
      \begin{algorithmic}[1]
            \Procedure{Tokenize}{$w$}
            \State $G \gets \langle\,\rangle$; \quad $i \gets 1$
            \While{$i \le |w|$}
            \State $\ell \gets \max \{\, k \in \{4, 3, 2\} : w[i\,{:}\,i{+}k{-}1] \in M_k \,\}$,
            \State \quad or $1$ if no multigraph matches
            \State $G \gets G \cdot w[i\,{:}\,i{+}\ell{-}1]$ ; \quad $i \gets i + \ell$
            \EndWhile
            \State \Return $G$
            \EndProcedure
      \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Rewrite Cascade}\label{subsec:cascade}

Graphemes are mapped to their Filipino equivalents through the priority-ordered
cascade of \cref{alg:nativize}, taking the first rule that succeeds:
context-sensitive orthographic rules, phonetic resolution for ambiguous vowels,
and context-free orthographic rules as the default.

\paragraph{Context-free rules}
Many English consonants bear an invariant relation to their nativized form, so
mapping reduces to direct substitution independent of context:
$\gr{ph}\mapsto\gr{f}$, $\gr{th}\mapsto\gr{t}$, $\gr{ee}\mapsto\gr{i}$,
$\gr{oo}\mapsto\gr{u}$, $\gr{kn}\mapsto\gr{n}$, $\gr{wr}\mapsto\gr{r}$, and
$\gr{dge}\mapsto\gr{j}$. The borrowed letters \gr{v}, \gr{z}, \gr{j} and the
digraph \gr{sh} are retained rather than folded onto native counterparts,
following the extended alphabet of~\cref{sec:filipino_orthography}. A
context-free mapping is defined for every grapheme, including those normally
handled contextually; the cascade can therefore always fall back on one, and
repeated letters can be resolved by it.

\paragraph{Context-sensitive rules}
The context-sensitive rules are grouped by the shape of the current grapheme,
with the consonant group divided further by letter. Example cases include:
\begin{itemize}
      \item Repeated letters: a maximal run of identical graphemes is consumed whole and
            emits the context-free replacement of the first. The exception is \gr{cc}
            before \gr{e}, \gr{i}, \gr{y}, or \gr{ee}, which the \gr{c} rules take instead
            so that the two letters may map differently: ``accept'' $\mapsto$ \gr{aksept},
            ``success'' $\mapsto$ \gr{sakses}.
      \item Cases for \gr{c}: \(\gr{c}\cdot(\gr{e}\mid\gr{i}\mid\gr{y}\mid\gr{ee})
            \mapsto\gr{s}\); \(\gr{ck}\mapsto\gr{k}\) and \(\gr{cch}\mapsto\gr{k}\), both
            consuming two; otherwise \(\gr{c}\mapsto\gr{k}\).
      \item Silent \gr{e}: a word-final \gr{e} is dropped unconditionally, regardless of
            the preceding grapheme.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Phonetic Resolution}\label{subsec:phonetic_resolution}

Vowels are the difficult case. A single English vowel grapheme can spell
several phonemes, and the five-vowel Filipino system collapses those
distinctions; worse, unstressed vowels reduce to a schwa that the spelling does
not mark. We call a grapheme \emph{ambiguous} when its Filipino form cannot be
read off the spelling. When a context-sensitive rule does not settle such a
grapheme, the system resolves it from the pronunciation (\cref{alg:phonetic}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
      \caption{Phonetic Resolution}\label{alg:phonetic}
      \begin{algorithmic}[1]
            \Procedure{Phonetic}{$G, P, i$}
            \State $t \gets G[i]$
            \If{$t$ is not an ambiguous vowel} \Return \textbf{fail}
            \EndIf
            \If{$t$ continues a vowel cluster} \Return $(\langle\,\rangle, 1)$
            \EndIf
            \State $p \gets$ vowel phoneme of $t$ in $P$, or \textbf{fail} if none
            \If{$p$ is r-colored}
            \State \Return $(\langle t \rangle, 1)$ if medial, else $(\langle \gr{e}, \gr{r} \rangle, 1)$
            \EndIf
            \If{$p = \ipa{@}$} \Comment{schwa}
            \State \Return $(\langle \gr{o} \rangle, 1)$ if word-final and $t \in \{\gr{o}, \gr{u}\}$;
            \State \quad $(\langle \gr{e} \rangle, 1)$ if word-final; \textbf{fail} otherwise
            \EndIf
            \State $(r, d) \gets \Call{P2G}{p}$ \Comment{$d$: is $p$ a diphthong?}
            \If{$r = \langle \gr{a} \rangle$ \textbf{and} $t \in \{\gr{o}, \gr{u}\}$}
            \State $r \gets \langle t \rangle$ \Comment{written letter overrides}
            \EndIf
            \State \Return $(r, \Call{Consumed}{G, i, d})$
            \EndProcedure
      \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The function \textsc{P2G} maps a single phoneme to its Filipino spelling.
Diphthongs become \gr{ey}, \gr{ay}, \gr{aw}, or \gr{oy}. The one exception is
\ipa{oU}, which becomes a single \gr{o}. For instance, the \ipa{eI} in
\gr{make} gives \gr{meyk}. A lax monophthong takes the nearest Filipino simple
vowel, so the \ipa{\ae} of \gr{cat} gives \gr{a}. When that nearest vowel is
\gr{a} but the written letter is \gr{o} or \gr{u}, the written letter is kept
instead. R-colored vowels and word-final schwas are spelled directly. A medial
r-colored vowel, one followed by \gr{r} and at least one further grapheme,
keeps its written letter; elsewhere it is spelled \gr{er}. A word-final schwa
is spelled \gr{o} after a written \gr{o} or \gr{u}, and \gr{e} after any other
letter. A schwa in any other position fails, falling through to the
context-free rules by design.

\subsection{Output Normalization}\label{subsec:normalization}

\textsc{Normalize} cleans up artifacts left where independent rules meet at a
boundary. First, adjacent identical graphemes emitted by different rules are
collapsed to one: a rule emitting \gr{d} directly before another emits \gr{dy}
produces \gr{ddy}, which collapses to \gr{dy}. Second, phonotactic cluster
repair inserts an epenthetic \gr{o} to break up disallowed word-final
consonant-liquid clusters derived from English schwas.
