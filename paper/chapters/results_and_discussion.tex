\section{Results and Discussion}

The system was evaluated on 2,319 English--Filipino loanword pairs.
\Cref{tab:CER} reports overall CER under four vowel-equivalence conditions,
comparing the proposed IPA-guided system against an ablation baseline in which
the phonetic vowel resolution stage is disabled. The ablation retains all
context-sensitive and context-free orthographic mappings, but maps each vowel grapheme
to its identity.

    \centering
    \caption{Overall CER (\%) under four vowel-equivalence conditions.}
    \label{tab:CER}
    \begin{tabular}{l|cccc}
        \hline
        \textbf{System} & \textbf{5-vowel} & \textbf{\emph{e}~=~\emph{i}~=~\emph{y}} & \textbf{\emph{o}~=~\emph{u}} & \textbf{3-vowel} \\
        \hline
        Baseline        & 17.73            & 14.50                                   & 17.42                        & 14.19            \\
        IPA-guided      & 5.14             & 4.14                                    & 5.05                         & 4.05             \\

        Reduction       & 12.59            & 10.36                                   & 12.37                        & 10.14             \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}

Under strict five-vowel evaluation the IPA-guided system achieved 5.14\% CER, a
71\% relative reduction from the 17.73\% baseline. Relaxing (\emph{e},
\emph{i}, \emph{y}) equivalence reduced CER to 4.14\%, while (\emph{o},
\emph{u}) relaxation yielded 5.05\%; combining both produced 4.05\%. The larger
gain from (\emph{e}, \emph{i}, \emph{y}) relaxation reflects the prevalence of
schwa-derived ambiguity: English unstressed vowels reduce to /\textipa{@}/,
which can surface as any of the five Filipino vowels.

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Manual inspection of the 856 character-level edits under the relaxed 3-vowel
scoring reveals four dominant failure modes.

\subsubsection{Unstressed Vowel Ambiguity}

The largest error source is the resolution of English unstressed vowels,
particularly the schwa /\textipa{@}/, to Filipino, whose orthography has no
dedicated reduced-vowel character. Under 3-vowel evaluation, vowel-to-vowel
substitutions dominate the error ranking: \emph{a}~\(\mapsto\)~\emph{o}
accounts for 46 errors, \emph{e}~\(\mapsto\)~\emph{a} for 37,
\emph{o}~\(\mapsto\)~\emph{a} for 27, and \emph{a}~\(\mapsto\)~\emph{e} for 23,
totaling over 133 vowel substitutions. Because the (\emph{a}, \emph{o})
contrast persists even after collapsing (\emph{e}, \emph{i}, \emph{y}) and
(\emph{o}, \emph{u}), the ambiguity is structural: the rule-based pipeline
cannot resolve it without lexical stress information or a pronunciation
dictionary lookup.

\subsubsection{Incomplete Glide Insertion}

Filipino phonotactics requires semivowel glides /w/ and /j/ to break vowel
hiatus and to close certain diphthongs. The current rules handle several common
environments but miss 42 \emph{w}-insertion and 40 \emph{y}-insertion sites
under 5-vowel evaluation. Errors cluster in two contexts: hiatus resolution,
where adjacent vowels require an intervening glide For instance,
``tiara''~\(\mapsto\)~``tiara'' instead of ``tiyara''. These gaps are
addressable through additional context conditions for vowel-initial suffixes
and word-final sequences.

\subsubsection{G2P Propagation Errors}

Some failures originate not in the transducer but in the upstream
grapheme-to-phoneme module. An incorrect intermediate representation cascades
directly into the output: ``chlorpromazine'' yields
``klor\textbf{o}p\textbf{o}r\textbf{a}masin'' rather than the expected
``klorpromasin'', with the consonant cluster /pr/ erroneously broken by a
spurious vowel. Such cases are identifiable by their multi-character deviation
from the gold standard and disproportionately affect pharmacological inputs,
the longest and most phonotactically complex tokens in the evaluation set.

\subsubsection{Conventional Loanword Divergence}

A subset of errors arises where established Filipino borrowing conventions
override systematic phonetic predictions. The most prominent is the
\emph{f}~\(\mapsto\)~\emph{p} substitution of 19 errors: Filipino historically
lacked /f/, and many entrenched loanwords preserve the older bilabial mapping.
The system, applying modern rules that recognize /f/ as part of the extended
Filipino alphabet, retains the fricative in all positions.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three limitations warrant consideration. First, the rules were derived from
prescriptive sources and may not have captured informal nativization patterns in colloquial text. Second, the rule set is Filipino-specific;
porting to another target language requires new linguistic analysis. Third,
performance is bounded by G2P accuracy, as phonemization errors propagate
directly to the final output.
