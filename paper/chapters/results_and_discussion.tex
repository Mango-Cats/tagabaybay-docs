\section{Results and Discussion}

\Cref{CER} reports the overall Character Error Rate under the four evaluation
conditions described in \cref{ssec:evaluation_metrics}. The system was evaluated on
2,045 words across the four gold corpora.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption{Overall Character Error Rate (\%) under four vowel-equivalence conditions.}
    \label{CER}
    \begin{tabular}{lccc c}
        \hline
        \textbf{5-vowel} & \textbf{\emph{e} = \emph{i} = \emph{y}} & \textbf{\emph{o} = \emph{u}} & \textbf{3-vowel} \\
        \hline
        4.67 & 3.70 & 4.58 & 3.61 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Under the default (strict five-vowel) condition, the system achieved a CER of
4.67\%. Relaxing the \{e, i, y\} equivalence reduced the CER to 3.70\%, while 
the \{o, u\} relaxation yielded a fairly marginal reduction to 4.58\%. The close 
range of error rates suggest that remaining character errors are not majorly 
attributed to the \{e, i, y\} or \{o, u\} equivalences, contributing minimally 
to overall error. Combining both equivalences produced the lowest CER at 3.61\%,
producing a 1.06 percentage-point drop.

Because English is a stress-timed language, the phoneme correspondence for vowels is sometimes
ambiguous and nondeterministic. For example, some of the failures in our tests were: adhered
$\rightarrow$ adherd, fractured $\rightarrow$ fraksterd, and hemisphere  $\rightarrow$ hemisfer.
This ambiguity results in \emph{e}, \emph{y}, and \emph{i} errors being the most frequent ones.
This may explain why relaxing the \{e, i, y\} equivalence significantly reduces the CER.
