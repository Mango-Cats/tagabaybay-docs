a relaxed {\emph{e}, \emph{i}, \emph{y}} case, a relaxed {\emph{o}, \emph{u}} case,

The system was evaluated on X words across the five gold corpora.

To validate the effectiveness of the G2P process and grapheme-phoneme alignment
on the adaptation of vowel sounds, a comparison against a non-mapped set can be
made. In this set the G2P process was removed entirely and each vowel results
to itself without inferrence of any context-sensitive rules or IPA reference.

Under the strict five-vowel condition, the system achieved a CER of X\%.
Relaxing the (\emph{e}, \emph{i}, \emph{y}) equivalence reduced the CER to
X\%, while the (\emph{o}, \emph{u}) relaxation yielded a fairly marginal
reduction to X\%. Combining both equivalences produced the lowest CER at
X\%, producing a X percentage-point drop; a total of X word pairs were
wrong.

Comparing to the unmapped set, there is a X\% difference in the five-vowel
CER and a X\% difference in the three-vowel CER, validating the need for
G2P and context-sensitive rules to more accurately assess corresponding vowel
sounds. The close range of error rates for both tests suggest that remaining
character errors are not majorly attributed to the (\emph{e}, \emph{i},
\emph{y}) or (\emph{o}, \emph{u}) equivalences, contributing minimally to
overall error.

Because English is a stress-timed language, some pronunciations get reduced to
the schwa /\textipa{@}/ sound. This creates a bit of an ambiguity between vowel
sounds as it is a very neutral sound. This results in \emph{e}, \emph{y}, and
\emph{i} errors being the most frequent ones. This may explain why relaxing the
    {\emph{e}, \emph{i}, \emph{y}} equivalence significantly reduces the CER.

The phoneme correspondence for vowels is most of the time ambiguous, splitting
a vowel pronunciation into either its long or short version. Some examples:

This ambiguity results in a problem where a vowel must be mapped to multiple
different pronunciations. From the above examples, ``mall'' had an incorrect
ouput of ``mal'', when the expected was ``mol'', incorrectly using the short
version of \emph{a}. Another one is ``football'' with an incorrect output of
``futbal'', when the expected was ``futbol'', again incorrectly using the short
version of emph{a}. These are some examples that show the nondeterminism of
long and short vowels.
