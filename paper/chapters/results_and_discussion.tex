\section{Results and Discussion}

\Cref{CER} reports the overall Character Error Rate under the four evaluation
conditions described in \cref{ssec:evaluation_metrics}. The system was evaluated on
2,045 words across the four gold corpora.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \caption{Overall Character Error Rate (\%) under four vowel-equivalence conditions.}
    \label{CER}
    \begin{tabular}{lccc c}
        \hline
        \textbf{5-vowel} & \textbf{\emph{e} = \emph{i} = \emph{y}} & \textbf{\emph{o} = \emph{u}} & \textbf{3-vowel} \\
        \hline
        11.33 & 8.76 & 11.20 & 8.63 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Under the default (strict five-vowel) condition, the system achieved a CER of
11.33\%. Relaxing the \{e, i, y\} equivalence reduced the CER to 8.76\%, a
2.57 percentage-point drop that accounts for the majority of the improvement.
By contrast, the \{o, u\} relaxation yielded only a marginal reduction to
11.20\%, suggesting that o/u confusions contribute minimally to the overall
error. Combining both equivalences produced the lowest CER at 8.63\%.

Because English is a stressed-timed language, the schwa /\textipa{@}/ sound
makes the pronunciation of some vowels ambiguous, making \emph{e}, \emph{y},
and \emph{i} errors the most occurring ones. This is why relaxing the relaxing
the \{e, i, y\} equivalence significantly reduces the CER.
