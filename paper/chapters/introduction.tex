\section{Introduction}

The integration of foreign vocabulary into a native language is a frequent
consequence of language contact~\cite{haugen1950}. Borrowing is especially
prevalent in multilingual settings where speakers routinely alternate between
two or more languages~\cite{haugen1950,uffman2015}.

Loanword adaptation is a task where the borrower must determine how to render a
foreign word using the sound system of the borrowing language. This often
presents a classic ``too-many-solutions'' problem~\cite{kang2011}, where
languages have multiple ways to resolve illegal phonotactic structures, such as
consonant deletion or vowel epenthesis.

However, loanword adaptation is not a purely phonetic phenomenon. Literature
has demonstrated that the orthographic form of the source word can also
systematically influence the adapted output~\cite{vendelin2006,
    kang2011,daland2015}. Vendelin and Peperkamp (2006) showed experimentally that
when speakers were presented with nonwords accompanied by their written forms,
their adaptations differed significantly from those based on auditory input
alone, confirming the independent role of spelling. This dominance of
orthography can often be attributed to the sociolinguistic setting,
particularly when words enter the language primarily through written
texts~\cite{uffman2015}. Furthermore, recent studies demonstrate that lower L2
proficiency combined with high spelling similarity causes borrowers to
systematically rely on reading rules over sound~\cite{wang2025}. Thus, the
adaptation process generally cannot be attributed to phonetic or phonological
representation alone; rather, it may draw on phonological, phonetic,
morphological, and orthographic information simultaneously~\cite{kang2011}.

Because borrowers must often first interpret the foreign orthography, map it to
native sounds, and finally apply phonetic repairs to illegal syllable
structures, we can model loanword adaptation as a sequential, multi-layered
process. While data-driven machine learning models can be utilized for this
task, they necessitate substantial parallel corpora to learn the underlying
mapping. This requirement presents a significant barrier, as large-scale
parallel datasets of source words and their adapted forms are often
unavailable. This data gap is particularly evident in low-resource languages,
precluding the straightforward application of these machine learning methods.

With this in mind, rule-based approaches become an attractive alternative.
Because loanword phenomena are often well-documented and guided by official
orthographic rules, this multi-layered process can be explicitly encoded as a
sequential algorithm. To automate adaptation, we propose a methodology that
embeds known linguistic rules directly into a Finite-State Transducer (FST)
framework~\cite{mao2016}. The primary computational advantage of FSTs in this
context is composition, which allows us to cascade multiple independent rule
sets—representing the various orthographic and phonological layers—into a
single, efficient model. By operating independently of training corpora, the
proposed system remains transparent, interpretable, and strictly consistent
with established linguistic descriptions. Ultimately, the underlying
computational engine is fundamentally language-agnostic, demonstrating its
potential applicability across diverse linguistic contexts.

In this study, we utilize Filipino as a compelling test case for
addressing this gap. Having been shaped by American influence, the language
draws heavily from both English, particularly in domains such as education,
medicine, commerce, and public administration~\cite{baklanova2017}. A simple
illustration is the concept of \emph{school}: a speaker may use the native
Filipino word \emph{paaralan} or the English-derived \emph{iskul} (from
\emph{school}).

In summary, the specific contributions of this study are as follows: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-resource methodology for loanword adaptation.} We demonstrate a viable approach for computational modeling in low-resource settings by directly encoding established linguistic rules, circumventing the need for large-scale parallel training corpora, and validating our model's accuracy against a standard dictionary corpus.
    \item \textbf{Filipino as a computational test case.} We utilize Filipino to evaluate the proposed methodology, deploying FSTs to automatically generate orthographically well-formed adaptations of English loanwords and confirming the framework's overall viability.
\end{itemize}
