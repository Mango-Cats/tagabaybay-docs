\section{Introduction}

Language contact commonly results in the borrowing of foreign lexical
items. Borrowed words rarely enter unchanged; instead, they undergo
\emph{nativization}, adapting to the phonological, morphological, and
orthographic conventions of the receiving language~\cite{haugen1950}.
This process is especially pervasive in multilingual and literacy-rich
settings, where foreign vocabulary is encountered through both spoken and
written channels~\cite{haugen1950,uffman2015}.

Among its dimensions, \emph{orthographic nativization}, the adaptation of
a loanword's spelling to the writing conventions of the borrowing
language, has received limited computational attention despite its
importance for consistent text processing in multilingual contexts. We
formulate orthographic nativization as a grapheme-to-grapheme transduction
task: given a source-language spelling, produce the single standardized
spelling prescribed by the borrowing language's orthographic norms.
Empirical work shows that spelling systematically influences adaptation
outcomes, that borrowers rely on orthographic cues when phonetic input is
ambiguous, and that orthographic dominance is strongest when loanwords
enter primarily through written discourse~\cite{vendelin2006,daland2015,wang2025,uffman2015}.

Computational modeling of this task must therefore encode both graphemic
correspondences and phonotactic repair strategies reflected in spelling
conventions. Constraint-based phonological frameworks such as Optimality
Theory provide explanatory accounts of adaptation but are difficult to
operationalize computationally due to complex constraint interaction and
limited interpretability~\cite{idsardi2006}. Purely data-driven approaches
face a complementary limitation: they require large parallel corpora of
source--nativized forms, which are typically unavailable for low-resource
languages. A prioritized cascade of rewrite rules offers a practical
middle ground, enabling transparent, linguistically grounded modeling
independent of training data~\cite{mao2016}.

We evaluate this rule-cascade architecture using Filipino as a test case.
Filipino is well-suited to this role because it borrows extensively from
English across domains such as education, medicine, commerce, and public
administration~\cite{baklanova2017}, and because official nativization
guidelines are codified in the Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino's
\emph{Manwal sa Masinop na Pagsulat}~\cite{almario2014}. These properties
allow the construction and systematic evaluation of a deterministic
rule-based model grounded in prescriptive orthographic standards.
Although the specific rules are language-dependent, the underlying
priority-cascade architecture is intended to be transferable to other
borrowing contexts with documented spelling conventions.

In summary, this study makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A rule-cascade framework for low-resource orthographic nativization.}
          We formalize orthographic nativization as prioritized grapheme-level
          rewrite transduction, demonstrating that prescriptive linguistic
          rules can be operationalized as an interpretable computational
          pipeline without large parallel corpora.

    \item \textbf{An empirical case study on English--Filipino borrowing.}
          Using Filipino as a computational testbench, we implement and
          evaluate the proposed framework on real loanword data, showing its
          practical effectiveness while identifying systematic error sources.
\end{itemize}
