\section{Introduction}

The integration of foreign vocabulary into a native language is a frequent
consequence of language contact~\cite{haugen1950}. Borrowing is especially
prevalent in multilingual settings where speakers routinely alternate between
two or more languages~\cite{haugen1950,uffman2015}.

Loanword adaptation is a task where the borrower must determine how to render a
foreign word using the sound system of the borrowing language. This often
presents a classic ``too-many-solutions'' problem~\cite{kang2011}, where
languages have multiple ways to resolve illegal phonotactic structures, such as
consonant deletion or vowel epenthesis.

However, loanword adaptation is not a purely phonetic phenomenon. Literature
has demonstrated that the orthographic form of the source word can also
systematically influence the adapted
output~\cite{vendelin2006,kang2011,daland2015}. Spelling has an independent role in loanword adaptation, as demonstrated by experimental findings showing that speakers' adaptations of nonwords differ significantly when written forms are provided alongside auditory input~\cite{vendelin2006}.
This dominance of orthography can often be attributed to the sociolinguistic
setting, particularly when words enter the language primarily through written
texts~\cite{uffman2015}. Furthermore, recent studies demonstrate that lower L2
proficiency combined with high spelling similarity causes borrowers to
systematically rely on reading rules over sound~\cite{wang2025}. Thus, the
adaptation process generally cannot be attributed to phonetic or phonological
representation alone; rather, it may draw on phonological, phonetic,
morphological, and orthographic information simultaneously~\cite{kang2011}.

Because borrowers must often first interpret the foreign orthography, map it to
native sounds, and finally apply phonetic repairs to illegal syllable
structures, loanword adaptation can be modeled as a sequential, multi-layered
process. While data-driven machine learning models can be applied to this task,
they require substantial parallel corpora to learn the underlying mapping. This
requirement presents a significant barrier, as large-scale parallel datasets of
source words and their adapted forms are often unavailable. This data gap is
particularly acute in low-resource languages, precluding the straightforward
application of machine learning methods.

With this in mind, rule-based approaches become an attractive alternative.
Because loanword phenomena are often well-documented and governed by official
orthographic standards, the multi-layered adaptation process can be explicitly
encoded as a deterministic algorithm. We propose a methodology that embeds
known linguistic rules directly into a cascade of context-sensitive rewrite
rules~\cite{mao2016}, organized by specificity: context-sensitive orthographic
rules take priority over context-free ones, and phonetic evidence from a
grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) component is consulted when orthography alone is
insufficient to determine the correct adapted form. By operating independently
of training corpora, the proposed system remains transparent, interpretable,
and strictly consistent with the established spelling conventions of the
borrowing language. The underlying rule architecture is designed to be
transferable: while the rules themselves are language-specific, the
priority-cascade framework can in principle be instantiated for other
well-documented borrowing contexts.

In this study, we use Filipino as a compelling test case for addressing this
gap. Having been shaped by prolonged American influence, the language draws
heavily from English, particularly in domains such as education, medicine,
commerce, and public administration~\cite{baklanova2017}. A simple illustration
is the concept of \emph{school}: a speaker may use the native Filipino word
\emph{paaralan} or the English-derived \emph{iskul}. The adaptation rules
governing such borrowings are codified in the Komisyon sa Wikang Filipino's
\emph{Manwal sa Masinop na Pagsulat}~\cite{almario2014}, providing a principled,
authoritative basis for a rule-based system.

In summary, the specific contributions of this study are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-resource methodology for loanword adaptation.} We
          demonstrate a viable approach for computational modeling in
          low-resource settings by directly encoding established linguistic
          rules, circumventing the need for large-scale parallel training
          corpora, and validating our model's accuracy against a standard
          dictionary corpus.
    \item \textbf{Filipino as a computational test case.} We deploy a
          rule-based cascade algorithm to automatically generate
          orthographically well-formed Filipino adaptations of English
          loanwords, confirming the framework's overall viability and
          identifying its current limitations.
\end{itemize}
