\section{Introduction}

Borrowed words rarely enter a language unchanged. When English loanwords are
adopted into Filipino, they must be adapted to conform to Filipino's
orthographic conventions, a process known as \emph{orthographic nativization}.
This computational task requires generating the standardized written form of a
loanword according to the recipient language's spelling rules. For example,
``computer'' becomes \emph{kompyuter}, and ``basketball'' becomes
\emph{basketbol}. Despite its practical importance for text normalization,
spell-checking, and corpus construction in multilingual natural language
processing, orthographic nativization has received considerably less
computational attention than the well-studied problems of phonological and
morphological adaptation~\cite{kang2011,uffmann2015,wang2025,daland2015}.

Filipino provides an ideal testbed for orthographic nativization. The language
borrows extensively from English across education, medicine, commerce, and
public administration~\cite{martin2020,baklanova2017}, and official
nativization guidelines are codified in the prescriptive manual by the Komisyon
sa Wikang Filipino~\cite{almario2014}. These properties enable systematic
evaluation against standardized orthographic norms. Furthermore, Filipino is
typologically distinct from English in phonological inventory, syllable
structure, and orthographic depth~\cite{llamzon1966,schachter1972,llamzon1997}.
This typological distance ensures that the proposed system must handle
substantial graphemic and phonotactic transformations, providing a rigorous
test of the approach.

Our method draws on prior work in computational loanword modeling while
addressing a distinct generation problem. Finite-State Transducer (FST)
with Optimality-Theoretic constraint ranking have successfully modeled
the morpho-phonological transformations of Arabic loanwords entering Swahili
for bilingual lexicon generation~\cite{tsvetkov2015}, and similar FST
approaches have captured much of the phonological adaptation from English into
Japanese~\cite{mao2016}. More broadly, nativization in loanword phonology has
been shown to be systematic and rule-governed across typologically diverse
languages~\cite{kang2011}. However, these studies focus on modeling
\emph{phonological} patterns (the abstract sound structure of adaptation)
rather than orthographic generation. As documented
in~\cite{wang2025,daland2015}, phonological and orthographic nativization are
not equivalent: a phonological model produces an abstract pronunciation
representation, while an orthographic nativization system must produce a
concrete character string that obeys the recipient's spelling rules.

The proposed system performs orthographic nativization through a four-stage
pipeline: graphemic tokenization, rule-based grapheme mapping, phonetic
resolution via Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) lookup, and output normalization.
Rules are hand-authored from prescriptive guidelines~\cite{almario2014} and
linguistic descriptions of Filipino
phonology~\cite{llamzon1966,schachter1972,llamzon1997,tayao2004,magpale2024},
making the approach transparent and linguistically grounded. By deriving
rewrites from existing linguistic resources rather than requiring parallel
training corpora, the method is particularly suited to low-resource scenarios
where annotated loanword data are unavailable.

This work makes three contributions. First, we formalize orthographic
nativization as a rewrite cascade, a priority-ordered sequential evaluation of
rule types over a token sequence where the first matching rule determines the
output for each token. We demonstrate that prescriptive linguistic rules can be
operationalized into an effective computational pipeline without large parallel
corpora, achieving 5.14\% character error rate under strict evaluation and
4.05\% when accounting for Filipino's allographic vowel variation. Second, we
compare the pipeline against a regex baseline, an ortho-only ablation, and
zero-shot LLM prompting, showing that the rewrite cascade
outperforms all three. Third, we provide an empirical case study on
English-Filipino borrowing using a newly constructed gold standard of 2,319
loanword pairs, identifying systematic error patterns and validating the
utility of phonetic disambiguation for vowel adaptation.
